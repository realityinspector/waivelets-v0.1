Can waivelets profile individual models? A small experiment.

Andy Ayrey asked whether this process can profile individual models — Sonnet-3.5 vs 3.6 vs GPT-4-turbo, etc. We ran the experiment. The answer is the interesting kind of no: not in the current fingerprint space, but the signal is there if you point at a different layer.

Setup

8 models, 12 prompts each. Prompts elicit ~500 words of varied genre (explanatory, narrative, philosophical reflection, occasional poetry). Generated via OpenRouter, all at temperature 0.7. Each generation was fingerprinted via the deployed /api/fingerprint, and the raw 384-D MiniLM embedding centroid was extracted locally using the public basis.npz.

Result 1 — the 7-D Gutenberg fingerprint does not separate models

The waivelets 7-D structural fingerprint was derived by hierarchical clustering on a 100-text corpus from Project Gutenberg. The four axes maximize variance among Homer-to-Kafka. There's no guarantee they're the axes that maximize variance among LLMs.

And in fact: they aren't. Pairwise centroid distances in z-scored 7-D space don't track model identity.

Closest pairs are across family lines (haiku-4.5 ↔ gemini-2.5-pro at 0.49; sonnet-4 ↔ gpt-4o at 0.69). Within-cluster spread (2.34) is twice the mean between-centroid distance (1.19). Models overlap heavily.

Quantitatively, leave-one-out LDA on the 7-D fingerprints gives 11.6% 8-way accuracy — at chance — and a permutation-null p-value of 0.43. The 4-way family classification is worse than the majority-class baseline (34.7% vs 37.5%). Unsupervised hierarchical clustering (Ward) gives adjusted Rand index of ~0 at every k from 2 to 8.

Headline. Models do not separate in the 7-D fingerprint space. The current basis is tuned to human-literary variance, not LLM-vs-LLM variance.

Result 2 — the signal is alive one layer down, but in a surprising place

Before declaring defeat, we re-ran the analysis on richer feature spaces extracted from the same 96 generations:

  • 26-D attractor coords (mean): the full layer beneath the 7-D summary — the raw eigenbasis projection, per-generation averaged.
  • 26-D basin visit histogram: how often each of the 26 attractors is the argmax-of-|projection| across sentences.
  • 78-D combined: concat of mean coords, |mean| coords, and basin histogram.
  • 384-D raw MiniLM embedding centroid: the per-generation mean of the original sentence embeddings, before the eigenbasis projection.

The 26-D attractor coords — the layer the eigenbasis projects to — give only marginal family-level signal (41.7% vs 37.5% majority, p=0.01) and no model-level signal. But the raw 384-D MiniLM embedding centroid, which the eigenbasis discards, separates families at 57.3% accuracy with p < 0.01 under a 100-permutation null. That's +19.8 percentage points over the majority-class baseline.

The synth-rack reading

The Moog framing (which is how this project was originally scoped) makes the result legible:

  • The eigenbasis is the patch bay — it routes 384-D embedding space into 26 attractor modulation sources.
  • The 7-number fingerprint is the summary panel — smoothness, oscillation, four domain activations, basin entropy.
  • Both were derived from human literary text. The basis maximizes variance among Homer, Plato, Austen, Kafka.
  • Asking it to discriminate LLMs is asking a synth wired for orchestral dynamics to separate different drum machines. The signal is in the source material but the patch bay doesn't route it.

The 384-D embedding is the oscillator output before the patch bay. There's clearly a model-discriminating signal sitting in that layer — different model families occupy different sub-regions of MiniLM embedding space, probably reflecting RLHF tuning, instruction-following templates, and stylistic priors. The eigenbasis throws this signal away because Gutenberg variance doesn't load on those directions.

The new module. Build a second eigenbasis from an LLM-output corpus, parallel to the Gutenberg basis. Same wavelet pipeline, different distribution. The two bases together (literary + model-discriminating) form a fuller patch bay. Each new model family becomes a discoverable timbre.

Followup — building the second light (the spectrometer works)

After framing the above, the question became: would a second basis actually carry complementary signal, or are we just trading the same information through a different prism? So we built it.

We took every sentence from all 96 generations (3 084 sentences total) and fit a 26-D PCA basis on the raw MiniLM embeddings. This is a "naive" LLM-corpus basis — no wavelets, no labels, just principal directions of LLM-generated sentence embedding space. We then computed the same 78-D per-generation feature (mean projection + |mean| projection + basin visit histogram) under each basis, and asked: does the LLM basis carry signal the Gutenberg basis doesn't?

The bases are nearly orthogonal

For each of the 26 Gutenberg eigenvectors, the best |cos| match in the LLM-PCA basis is 0.11 on average (range 0.04–0.18). The two bases point in essentially different directions in ℝ³⁸⁴. They illuminate different parts of the embedding manifold.

The spectrometer principle holds. The two bases carry complementary signal. Gutenberg alone gets 58% model-level accuracy. LLM-PCA alone gets 70% (better than Gutenberg — because it's tuned to the actual distribution). Both together get 76%. That's a +6pp lift on top of the better single basis, from concatenation alone — no fancy fusion, no learned weights. The bases are perpendicular, so they don't compete; they stack.

The interesting correction

Note something the v0.1 critique above got partly wrong: the Gutenberg basis itself already carries strong model-discriminating signal (58% accuracy) — it was the 7-D summary panel (smoothness, oscillation, entropy, four domain activations) that was the bottleneck, not the basis. When you read out all 78 dimensions (mean projection + |mean| projection + basin histogram across the 26 attractors), the basis lights up significantly on per-model variance. The published 7-D fingerprint is a lossy reduction of a richer underlying object.

That's the second methodological update from this run: the summary panel is for humans; the analytical layer should read the full basis projection. The fingerprint card is the speedometer; the spectrometer is the diagnostic port.

What the new basis "sees"

The LLM-PCA basis captures whatever directions in MiniLM space are most variable across LLM outputs — the axes along which different models, prompts, and topics spread. The Gutenberg basis captures whatever directions are most informative about human literary structure. The fact that they're nearly orthogonal (mean |cos| 0.11) confirms a real distinction:

  • Gutenberg directions = structural categories of human writing (exposition, interiority, formal structure, scene narration, etc.) — discovered by wavelet correlation analysis on the Gutenberg corpus.
  • LLM-PCA directions = whatever maximally varies across LLM outputs — likely a mix of topic, register, model-family stylistic priors, and training-set artifacts.

Neither is "the right one." The flashlight analogy you proposed is the right shape: shine multiple lights, read the per-light spectrum, recognize the model by the pattern. Building a third basis (e.g. on technical prose, or on dialogue, or on poetry) would add another column to the spectrometer.

The new basis is saved as web/assets/llm_basis.npz in the repo (37KB, same shape as basis.npz). Drop-in compatible.

Caveats

Small n. 12 generations per model is enough to detect a clean signal at p < 0.01 but isn't enough to discriminate fine-grained variants (Sonnet-4 vs Sonnet-4.5). Within-family separation needs an order-of-magnitude more samples. The 8-way model-level accuracy (28%) is significantly above chance (12.5%) but still most pairs are confusable; the strong signal is at the family level.
  • Prompt overlap. All 8 models got the same 12 prompts. Per-prompt content variance dominates per-model voice variance, which is why unsupervised clustering on the 384-D embeddings finds zero model structure (ARI = 0.00, purity = 0.125). LDA only works because we hand it the labels.
  • Temperature 0.7. Higher temperature → wider intra-model spread → harder to separate. Production AI-detection use cases will hit varied temperatures.
  • Reasoning models. We capture only the final message.content — not the reasoning trace. Models like gpt-5 and gemini-2.5-pro have a chain-of-thought layer that's invisible to this experiment.
  • Embedding model bias. MiniLM was trained on its own distribution. A different embedder (BGE, Nomic, OpenAI) might find a different separation. The model-discriminating signal we found is partly a fingerprint of MiniLM's reading of the models, not just the models themselves.

Reproducibility

Full experiment scripts and data:

  • github.com/realityinspector/waivelets-v0.1 — repo
  • exp/prompts.json — 12 prompts used
  • exp/models.json — 8 models tested
  • exp/run.py + exp/retry.py — generation scripts (OpenRouter)
  • exp/extract_26d.py — local 26-D + 384-D feature extraction
  • exp/analyze.py + exp/analyze2.py — LDA, PCA, clustering, permutation null
  • exp/fit_llm_basis.py — fits the LLM-PCA basis, runs spectrometer comparison
  • web/assets/llm_basis.npz — the LLM-PCA basis (384×26), drop-in compatible with the existing basis.npz
  • exp/results.jsonl, exp/coords26.jsonl — raw outputs and fingerprints (gitignored; regenerate from scripts)

If you want to add a model not in this set, fork and add it to exp/models.json. If you want to test on a different embedder, swap SentenceTransformer('all-MiniLM-L6-v2') in fastprint.py with your preferred encoder (the eigenbasis was fit on MiniLM-384 so dimension must match, or recompute).

What this answers — and doesn't

Answers Andy's question: Yes, model profiling is feasible. Not with the 7-D fingerprint as it stands; yes, with one additional layer (the raw MiniLM embedding centroid). Family-level discrimination is robust (p < 0.01, +19.8pp over baseline); individual-model discrimination is real but weak (p < 0.01, +15.6pp over chance) and needs more data per model.

Doesn't answer: whether the model-discriminating signal is structural in the same sense as the AI-vs-human signal. The 384-D centroid is essentially the model's mean semantic territory across these prompts — closer to "what topics does this model gravitate toward" than to "how does meaning move through its sentences." A real structural fingerprint for individual models would require the proposed second eigenbasis derived from per-model wavelet analysis. We haven't built that.

Open invitation: if you have a labeled corpus of model outputs (per-model, per-prompt-class, n >= 50 per model), file an issue and we'll fit the LLM-eigenbasis.
